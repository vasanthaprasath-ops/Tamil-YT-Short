# Psychology of Attention, Retention, Sharing and Myth-Correction for a "Paati sonnadhu unmaiya?" Shorts Channel

Evidence grading used throughout: **[STRONG]** = peer-reviewed, large-N or replicated; **[MODERATE]** = peer-reviewed but single study / lab-only / small-N; **[WEAK]** = vendor blog, unverified platform stat, or pop-psychology. Channel implications are tagged **DO / DON'T**.

## (a) What drives sharing? (Berger & Milkman arousal work, STEPPS, social currency, identity, newer studies)

### Takeaway
The best-supported driver of sharing is **high-arousal emotion** (awe, surprise, anxiety, anger), with low-arousal sadness suppressing sharing; identity language (us vs them) is an even stronger engagement driver but is a double-edged tool. For family-WhatsApp sharing, the realistic levers are awe/surprise ("I never knew!"), mild useful anxiety ("my mother does this every day"), practical value, and in-group warmth — not outrage.

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Milkman (2012), JMR 49(2):192–205: ~7,000 NYT articles over 3 months tracked against the "most e-mailed" list, plus experiments. Positive content was more viral overall, but **high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) increased virality and low-arousal sadness decreased it**; experiments showed the effect runs through physiological activation. **[STRONG]** — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360292620129675648?lang=en)
- Practical utility and interest/surprise also predicted the most-e-mailed list in the same paper (controls in the regression) — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
- Berger (2011), *Psychological Science*, "Arousal increases social transmission of information": arousal induced by an unrelated task (even running in place) increased willingness to share content. **[MODERATE — lab]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611413294)
- Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021), PNAS: 2.73M Facebook/Twitter posts. **Posts about the out-group were shared ~2x as often as in-group posts**; out-group language was the strongest predictor of shares (~4.8x the effect of negative-affect words, ~6.7x moral-emotional words). Out-group words predicted "angry" reactions; **in-group language predicted "love" reactions**. Correlational; political content, US. **[STRONG, but correlational & political]** — [PMC8256037](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8256037); [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PNAS..11824292R/abstract)
- Robertson et al. (2023), *Nature Human Behaviour* 7:812–822: ~105,000 Upworthy headline A/B variants. **Each negative word raised click-through; positive words lowered it**. Surprise: **sadness, not anger, drove clicks** (note: this is *clicks*, not *shares* — contrasts with Berger & Milkman's sadness→less sharing). **[STRONG]** — [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/); [LMU open PDF](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/1/s41562-023-01538-4.pdf); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/a-particular-type-of-language-boosts-online-news-consumption-study-suggests/)
- Berger's **STEPPS** (Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories) comes from his trade book *Contagious* (2013). It is a synthesis framework, not a validated model; only the Emotion and (partly) Practical value parts rest directly on the JMR data above. **[WEAK as a model; components vary]** — framework summarized in [Berger & Milkman PDF context / Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=10141)
- WhatsApp specifically: a global study of viral WhatsApp content found health/fear-inducing alerts recur yearly, and that in India 60 of 158 misinformation posts had **already been fact-checked yet still circulated**; in-group correction was almost absent (one instance across India/Indonesia), attributed to social pressure/power dynamics of correcting people. **[MODERATE]** — [arXiv 2407.08172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.08172)
- Reuters Institute DNR 2025 (India, online English-speaking sample, n≈2,044 — not nationally representative): **YouTube 55% weekly news reach (highest of any market), WhatsApp 46%**; video, personality-driven news increasingly preferred in India. **[MODERATE, sample-skewed]** — [WION summary](https://www.wionews.com/technology/reuters-2025-digital-news-report-young-indians-prefer-ai-and-influencers-for-news-1750172305224/amp); [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/indians-biggest-consumers-of-ai-generated-news-most-comfortable-with-it-reuters-institute-report/2661648); sample caveat [ACJ](https://asianmedia.org/acj/home/event_details/223)

### Inferences
- **DO** engineer an *awe/surprise* beat ("adhu unmaiyave science-la proof irukku!") and a *practical value* beat (one actionable line the viewer can tell amma/appa). These are the two sharing levers best supported for non-political content.
- **DO** use *in-group warmth* (our paati, our house, our ooru) — Rathje's data links in-group language to "love" reactions, which suits family WhatsApp forwarding.
- **DON'T** build hooks on out-group mockery ("these foolish old people", "North Indians do X"). Out-group animosity boosts shares but invites angry reactions, alienates the elder/family audience you want forwarding the video, and is a brand risk.
- **DON'T** end on sadness/regret ("so we've been harming our kids for years…") — low arousal suppresses sharing per Berger & Milkman. Sadness may get *clicks* (Robertson) but the share moment should be activating.
- The WhatsApp "social pressure against correcting elders" finding is your opportunity: a neutral, respectful third-party video lets a grandchild correct a family myth *without personally confronting* paati — i.e., the video is a face-saving social tool. Frame and title episodes so forwarding them feels like sharing, not scolding.
- **DO** make the verdict card a standalone, screenshot-able frame (social currency + "public" in STEPPS terms) — inference, untested.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on *share* drivers specific to YouTube Shorts / TikTok in Tamil or Indian regional-language markets (2020–2026). Most short-video "virality" studies found were vendor analyses.
- No primary data on the rate at which Shorts are forwarded to WhatsApp.

## (b) Curiosity gaps and hooks (Loewenstein; question vs statement hooks)

### Takeaway
Curiosity (an information gap the viewer feels) reliably activates reward circuitry and improves memory for the answer and even for incidental content shown during the curious state. Evidence that **question-style hooks beat statements** exists but is thin (one small study); self-referencing questions ("do *you*…") did best.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein (1994), *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1):75–98: **information-gap theory** — curiosity arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and wants to know; it rises with prior partial knowledge (you need to know a bit to feel the gap). **[STRONG, foundational theory]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)
- Kang et al. (2009), *Psychological Science* 20:963–973: curiosity about trivia answers activated caudate (reward) and improved memory for answers; curiosity was highest at intermediate confidence (inverted-U). **[STRONG]** — [Citation via eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2zd605r7/qt2zd605r7.pdf?t=rttpgp)
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014), *Neuron* 84(2):486–496: high-curiosity states enhanced midbrain/nucleus-accumbens activity and improved memory both for the answer **and for unrelated incidental material (faces) shown while waiting**, tested immediately and a day later. **[STRONG]** — [PMC4252494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/); [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/96033)
- Counter-finding: a later paper reports curiosity states can *interfere* with memory for incidental scholastic facts — the incidental benefit is not universal. **[MODERATE]** — [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4MWzeNz9)
- Lai & Farbrot (2014), *Social Influence*: Twitter (6,000 followers) and Norwegian auction-site experiments. Question headlines got ~150% more clicks than statements; **self-referencing questions ~175%** (Twitter); on the auction site 137% vs 257%, but washing-machine question ads did *worse*. Critics note confounds between headline pairs. **[WEAK–MODERATE: single small study, effect varies by topic]** — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/are-you-more-likely-click-headlines-are-phrased-question); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140108112614.htm)
- A 2025 study of TikTok hook wording/sentiment and engagement exists (Ludovit et al., Sumy State Univ.), but effect sizes could not be verified. **[UNVERIFIED]** — [PDF](https://mmi.sumdu.edu.ua/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/04_A876-2025_Ludovit-et-al.pdf)

### Inferences
- The series title "Paati sonnadhu unmaiya?" is already a self-referencing question — keep it. **DO** open with a second-person question tied to the viewer's own household: "Saapta udane kulikka koodadhu-nu unga veetla sollirukkangala?" (Lai & Farbrot self-reference + Loewenstein partial-knowledge gap — every Tamil viewer already "half-knows" the rule).
- **DO** pick myths at intermediate certainty — ones viewers suspect *might* be true (Kang inverted-U). Rules that are obviously silly or obviously true generate less curiosity.
- **DO** hold the verdict until the end and state explicitly that it is coming ("mudivula verdict sollren — UNMAI, PAADHI UNMAI, illa POI?"). The open gap is what carries retention; Gruber suggests content shown *during* the gap (your science explanation) is better remembered.
- **DON'T** over-promise in the hook (clickbait). The gap must close with a real answer, or trust and repeat viewership suffer (inference; no direct study found on Shorts).

### Gaps
- No rigorous A/B evidence found comparing question vs statement **spoken/on-screen hooks in short video** specifically; headline studies are the nearest proxy.

## (c) Attention retention in short video (first-seconds drop-off, pacing, captions, faces, narration)

### Takeaway
Rigorous academic data on Shorts retention is scarce; most "first 3 seconds" statistics circulating online are vendor claims without traceable methodology. What is solid: YouTube exposes a **"Viewed vs Swiped away"** metric for Shorts (the first-moment decision), and **captions improve comprehension, attention and memory** (100+ studies). Treat pacing/cut-frequency numbers as folklore.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube Studio's Shorts report shows **"Viewed vs. Swiped away"** (share of feed impressions where the viewer kept watching); YouTube publishes **no official threshold**; third-party targets of 70–80% "viewed" are guidance only. **[MODERATE — metric real, benchmarks WEAK]** — [Creator Essentials glossary](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/); [Buffer guide](https://buffer.com/resources/the-creators-guide-to-youtube-shorts-analytics); [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/youtube-shorts-analytics-metrics-growth)
- "65% of people who watch the first 3 seconds watch at least 10" (attributed to Meta), "0.9-second attention span", "hook in first second lifts 2–3s retention from 63% to 81%", "68% of TikTok views start sound-off" — **all untraceable to primary sources**. **[WEAK — do not cite as fact]** — [compilation](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9); [Clutch](https://clutch.co/resources/what-makes-viewers-watch)
- Gernsbacher (2015), *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences*: review of **100+ empirical studies showing captions improve comprehension of, attention to, and memory for video**, for hearing viewers too. **[STRONG]** — [PDF](https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Video-Captions-Benefit-Everyone_2015.pdf)
- Eye-tracking study (n=168): watching **without sound increased cognitive load and reduced comprehension, immersion and enjoyment**, and increased reliance on subtitles. **[MODERATE]** — [arXiv record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/812736238183251968)
- Gruber et al. (2014): material presented during a curiosity state is better remembered — supports keeping the open question alive across the middle of the video. **[STRONG]** — [PMC4252494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/)
- Roozenbeek et al. (2022) showed that **short animated videos** run as YouTube pre-roll ads produced measurable learning (~5% better manipulation recognition) in a real, distracted feed environment — evidence that brief animation can teach in-feed. **[STRONG]** — [Univ. of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2022/august/misinformation-media-release.html); [PopSci](https://www.popsci.com/health/misinformation-inoculation-theory-videos/)

### Inferences
- **DO** treat the first frame as the hook: on-screen Tamil text of the myth-question + visual of the act (person lying head-north, turmeric on a cut) in frame 1, no logo/intro card. Measure with Viewed-vs-Swiped-away per video; compare your own videos against each other rather than chasing blog benchmarks.
- **DO** burn in large Tamil captions (plus Tanglish/English keywords if targeting diaspora) — strongest evidence in this section, and protects sound-off viewers.
- **DO** keep the verdict question visibly "pending" (e.g., a three-slot UNMAI / PAADHI UNMAI / POI meter on screen with the needle hidden) to sustain the curiosity state.
- Faceless format: no evidence found that a human face is necessary for retention in short video; a consistent animated Paati character can serve as the recurring "face" (inference — untested).
- **DON'T** design around specific cut-frequency rules ("cut every 1.5s"); none found with credible evidence. Test pacing yourself.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed eye-tracking or platform-published data found on Shorts first-second drop-off, cut frequency, or face vs faceless retention. YouTube/TikTok/Meta do not publish such attention data in citable form (only creator-help definitions).
- Could not verify the Ludovit et al. 2025 TikTok-hook effect sizes.

## (d) Correcting myths without backfiring or offending

### Takeaway
The "backfire effect" is **rare and unreliable** — current consensus says correct without fear. Best practice is a **fact-first structure, mention the myth once with a warning, explain *why* it's wrong (give an alternative explanation), and end on the fact**. In India, simple social corrections reduce belief (effects range from substantial to small), religious/cultural framing of a correction helped, while classroom-style media-literacy training did not reliably work. Prebunking via short animation has field evidence on YouTube.

### Cited Findings
- **Backfire is rare:** Wood & Porter (2019), *Political Behavior*, "The Elusive Backfire Effect": 5 experiments, >10,000 participants, 52 issues — **no issue produced backfire**. **[STRONG]** — [Swire-Thompson 2020 review PDF](https://www.bu.edu/ballab/pubs/swire-thompson2020.pdf); [Jason Collins summary](https://www.jasoncollins.blog/posts/is-there-a-backfire-effect)
- Swire-Thompson, DeGutis & Lazer (2020), *J. Applied Research in Memory and Cognition* 9:286–299: failures to replicate backfire are widespread even with identical items; **81% of reported backfire effects used a single unreliable measure**; worldview backfire found only in subgroup analyses. **[STRONG]** — [BU PDF](https://www.bu.edu/ballab/pubs/swire-thompson2020.pdf); [PMC9283209 (2022 follow-up)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9283209)
- **Debunking Handbook 2020** (22 scholars, consensus document): misinformation is sticky so pre-empt it; corrections reduce belief but a **continued influence effect** persists; **do not refrain from correcting out of fear of backfire**. Recommended structure: **FACT (lead with the truth if it is clear and sticky) → MYTH (warn before mentioning it, repeat it only once) → FALLACY (explain why it's wrong / how it misleads) → FACT (repeat the truth, ideally as an alternative causal explanation)**. **[STRONG — consensus]** — [CSSN page](https://cssn.org/the-debunking-handbook-2020-a-consensus-based-handbook-of-recommendations-for-correcting-or-preventing-misinformation/); [CSSN PDF](https://cssn.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/DB2020paper-1.pdf); [UNL record](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/scholcom/245/)
- "Truth sandwich" (truth–lie–truth) is George Lakoff's journalistic term for essentially the same shape; the Handbook's fact-myth-fallacy-fact is its evidence-backed version. **[framing term, not a separate study]**
- **Prebunking:** Roozenbeek et al. (2022), *Science Advances* (DOI 10.1126/sciadv.abo6254): 7 experiments, ~30,000 participants incl. a YouTube field test; short animations on manipulation techniques (emotional language, false dichotomies, scapegoating) as pre-roll ads raised recognition of manipulation ~5%, across political groups and education levels; cost <4p per view; durability unclear. **[STRONG]** — [Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2022/august/misinformation-media-release.html); [Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/inoculateexperiment); [The Swaddle](https://www.theswaddle.com/pre-bunking-fake-news-on-youtube-helped-curb-misinformation-shows-study)
- **India / WhatsApp:**
  - Badrinathan & Chauchard (2023), *Int. J. Press/Politics*, "I Don't Think That's True, Bro!": online experiment, ~5,100 Indians, mock WhatsApp threads incl. health claims. **Social corrections substantially reduced belief, even for beliefs anchored in group identity**; even an unsourced, low-sophistication doubt signal helped. **[STRONG]** — [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/952227/a-matter-of-fact-new-study-shows-how-indians-respond-to-mis-information-on-whatsapp); [AMiner record](https://aminer.cn/pub/645d0393d68f896efa9298b1/i-don-t-think-that-s-true-bro-social-corrections-of-misinformation)
  - Contradicting/qualifying: Altay, Badrinathan et al. (2025), *HKS Misinformation Review*: in India and Brazil, **short user corrections only slightly, often non-significantly**, reduced belief and sharing intent. **[STRONG — tempers the above]** — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/93635/); [American University](https://www-cdn.american.edu/sis/research/highlights/20250807-sumitra-badrinathan-user-corrections-misinformation.cfm)
  - Badrinathan & Chauchard, COVID religious-messaging experiment (India): **corrections that included religious frames reduced endorsement of misinformation**; religiosity correlated with higher misinformation belief. **[MODERATE–STRONG]** — [paper PDF](https://sumitrabadrinathan.github.io/Assets/paper-covid.pdf)
  - Badrinathan (2021), *APSR*: an in-person media-literacy field experiment in Bihar did **not** reliably improve people's ability to spot misinformation — generic "how to spot fakes" training is weak. **[STRONG]** — [Cambridge APSR](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/educative-interventions-to-combat-misinformation-evidence-from-a-field-experiment-in-india/A522EB5164406DE320647014946D31B3)
  - Indian WhatsApp viral-content study: corrections inside groups are near-absent due to social/power dynamics of correcting people (relevant to correcting elders). **[MODERATE]** — [arXiv 2407.08172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.08172)
  - Government AIKosh dataset of Hindi/Hinglish health myth/fact statements exists because health-misinfo resources are English-dominated — regional-language gap is real. — [AIKosh](https://aikosh.indiaai.gov.in/home/datasets/details/health_message_verifiers.html)

### Inferences
- **Backfire is not a reason to soften verdicts.** **DO** say POI clearly when the science is clear; the risk to manage is *offence and continued influence*, not backfire.
- **DO** structure every episode as Fact-Myth-Fallacy-Fact mapped onto the hook format: (1) Hook = question naming the rule (the one permitted myth mention, framed as a question, not an assertion); (2) "Why paati said it" = the *reasonable origin* (fallacy step, done respectfully — e.g., heavy meal + cold bath advice may come from post-meal drowsiness/blood-flow intuitions); (3) What science says (alternative explanation); (4) Verdict card that states the **true takeaway**, not the myth. The last spoken/written line should be the fact, e.g., "POI — aana saapta udane neenda neram neenja vendaam" rather than restating the myth.
- **DO** use PAADHI UNMAI generously and honestly — many elder rules encode a real kernel (turmeric's curcumin has lab-studied antimicrobial properties but kitchen turmeric on an open wound has hygiene risks). Crediting the kernel is the cultural-respect move *and* the Handbook's "alternative explanation".
- **DO** frame paati as wise-but-working-with-older-information ("avanga kaalathula theriyaadha science") — the correction is of the *rule*, not the *person*. Religious framing helped corrections in India; by analogy, aligning with values the audience holds (care for family, "paati wanted you safe") is likely to soften defensiveness (inference).
- **DON'T** put the myth as the dominant on-screen text at the end or as the thumbnail/title without a question mark — that maximizes familiarity with the false claim (continued influence).
- **DON'T** lecture "how to spot fake news" generically — Bihar media-literacy training didn't work; concrete, specific debunks + a recurring "why rules like this spread" micro-segment (prebunking of a technique, e.g., "fear + forward" pattern) has better evidence.
- The channel is literally a scalable "social correction": Badrinathan shows even a simple doubt signal helps; Altay 2025 shows effects can be small. Expect modest belief change per video; repetition across episodes matters.

### Gaps
- Handbook PDF text could not be fetched in this session (only the cover rendered); the Fact-Myth-Fallacy-Fact structure above is from the published Handbook as widely documented, not re-read here — verify wording at sks.to/db2020.
- No study found specifically on correcting **Tamil household/elder health beliefs** or on intergenerational correction effects in Tamil Nadu.
- Ecker et al. (2022) *Nature Reviews Psychology* review on misinformation drivers was not fetched; would strengthen this section.

## (e) Nostalgia, collective identity and regional/local pride as engagement drivers

### Takeaway
Nostalgia robustly increases **feelings of social connectedness** (replicated 12+ times in lab work), which plausibly fuels sharing with family; in-group language predicts "love" reactions. Direct evidence that nostalgia or regional pride boosts *shares/views* of short video was not found — it is a well-grounded inference, not a measured effect.

### Cited Findings
- Nostalgia → social connectedness is a **robust, replicated (>12 times)** finding (Wildschut, Sedikides et al.). **[STRONG — lab]** — [Southampton review PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/473959/2/1_s2.0_S2352250X22002664_main.pdf)
- Sedikides, Wildschut et al. (2016), *Emotion*: nostalgia fosters **self-continuity via social connectedness** (Experiments 1–4). **[STRONG — lab]** — [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/fda3787d-45c1-453a-816e-72dcdfe65794); [Winchester](https://winchester.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/nostalgia-fosters-self-continuity-uncovering-the-mechanism-social)
- Lasaleta, Sedikides & Vohs (2014), *J. Consumer Research*: nostalgia **reduced desire for money / increased willingness to spend**, mediated by social connectedness; lab experiments, not ad field tests. **[MODERATE]** — [Southampton PDF](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/~crsi/Lasaleta,%20Sedikides,%20and%20Vohs%202014.pdf)
- Nostalgia → gratitude via social connectedness (2025). **[MODERATE]** — [Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/508175/2/1-s2.0-S0191886925005653-main.pdf)
- In-group language predicts "love" reactions; out-group language predicts shares *and* anger (Rathje et al. 2021). **[STRONG, correlational]** — [PMC8256037](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8256037)
- Nostalgic advertising evidence is mostly qualitative (e.g., semiotic analysis of a Finnish coffee campaign), not audience-effect measurement. **[WEAK for engagement claims]** — [Helsinki](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/a5ac2b02-851a-4674-b634-db646868a993/content)

### Inferences
- **DO** make paati's world nostalgic and specific (thinnai, ammi kal, kolam, brass sombu, Sunday Doordarshan-era props) — triggers nostalgia → connectedness → "send this to the family group". The *Paati* frame is itself a nostalgia device.
- **DO** end with a prompt that converts connectedness into action: "Ungal paati enna solluvaanga? Comment pannunga / family group-la kelunga" — inference based on connectedness, untested.
- **City-identity series: DO** use in-group pride ("Madurai makkalukku mattum puriyum…", local food, dialect words); **DON'T** do city-vs-city put-downs (out-group animosity = shares + anger + comment-section fights; brand risk in a family-forward channel). Light, affectionate rivalry is a judgement call — no evidence found on where "playful" turns hostile.
- Collective identity also matters for corrections: Badrinathan & Chauchard found social corrections worked even for identity-anchored beliefs — so a myth tied to Tamil identity can be corrected if delivered as from *within* the in-group (Tamil voice, Tamil paati, Tamil science), not from outside.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence found that regional/local pride or nostalgia increases **views or shares of short-form video**; all engagement implications here are inferred from lab connectedness findings and the Rathje correlational data.
- No India- or Tamil-specific nostalgia/media studies found.

## (f) Do verdict formats, quizzes and "guess before the answer" devices increase engagement/learning?

### Takeaway
Yes for **learning and curiosity**: asking people to predict/guess before a reveal increases curiosity and pupil-measured arousal (Brod 2019), and even wrong guesses improve later memory of the answer when feedback follows immediately (pretesting effect, Kornell 2009; Richland 2009). No study was found testing verdict/rating formats on short-video *engagement metrics* specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Brod & Breitwieser (2019), *npj Science of Learning*, "Lighting the wick in the candle of learning": generating a **prediction** before seeing a trivia answer **raised curiosity** vs generating an example; **pupil dilation was larger** during anticipation and at the reveal. Higher curiosity went with better memory, but prediction vs example conditions **did not differ in overall memory**. **[MODERATE]** — [peDOCS PDF](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2022/25224/pdf/NPJ_2019_4_Brod_et_al_Lighting_the_wick_in_the_candle_of_learning_A.pdf)
- Theobald, Galeano-Keiner & Brod (2022): prediction confidence relates to curiosity and surprise at the answer (expectancy violation). **[MODERATE]** — [Uni Trier PDF](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/SUK/Theobald_et_al._2022_Predicting_vs._Guessing.pdf)
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), *JEP:LMC* 35(4):989–998: **guessing before the answer is shown improved later recall vs studying for the same time — even when the guess was wrong**. **[STRONG]** — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-pluses-of-getting-it-wrong/)
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), *JEP: Applied*: pretesting on unlearned material improved learning; benefit came from **attempting** answers, not just seeing questions. **[STRONG]** — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/getting-it-wrong/)
- 2020 *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* review: pretesting works best when **corrective feedback is immediate** after the error. **[STRONG]** — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-020-01810-y.pdf)
- Kang et al. (2009): curiosity peaks at intermediate confidence — guesses on uncertain items maximize curiosity. **[STRONG]** — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2zd605r7/qt2zd605r7.pdf?t=rttpgp)

### Inferences
